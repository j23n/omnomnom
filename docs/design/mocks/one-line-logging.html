Omnomnom · design mock · 3 Oct 2026

One-line logging

Mocks for the redesign that replaces gram-accurate per-food entry with one line of natural language. Day-level totals, accurate to roughly ±15–20%, read as a 7-day rolling average over months. Everything below follows from that target resolution, including the things that stop working because of it.

This is a document to argue with, not a gallery. Every screen carries the decision it demonstrates and what was left out on purpose; every journey carries its tap count and seconds against the product targets. The last section lists the places where the premise broke down when I tried to draw it.

  • Target resolution

    Day totals, ±15–20%, read as a 7-day average. Not meal accuracy.

  • Primary input

    One typed or dictated line. No search screen, no gram field by default.

  • Grounding

    Every value comes from a Ciqual/BLS row. The model names foods, never numbers.

  • Repetition

    Remembered invisibly from what was typed. No saved meals to name or curate.

  • Incompleteness

    Logged, assumed and missing are three different things and stay visible.

01 — Today

The day, and nothing about how the day went

Energy, protein and fibre are the headline; the other five nutrients still reach Apple Health but have no place on this screen. The composer is pinned at the bottom, in reach of a thumb, because the plan's one-handed requirement is the only thing on Today that is a hard target.

The first pair is deliberately shown in both appearances at once; everything after it follows the Screens switch above. Every frame's content scrolls inside the device, so what sits below the fold can be read without leaving the page.

02 — The composer

The whole redesign rests on one field

The composer has to do three things at once: take a sentence, show what it understood before the user commits, and stay out of the way when it understood everything. The understanding is shown as chips above the field, built as the line is typed, each one a food plus an amount.

Chips are deliberately not tinted. Tangerine means "you can act on this"; the chips are the app reporting what it read, so they are neutral. The only tinted things on this screen are the mic and the Log button, both of which do something.

03 — The resolution sheet

Five states, three tiers, no colour

A row on this sheet is in one of five states. From history — the food and the amount come back from the last time, no model involved. Checked and certain — retrieved from the bundled tables and confirmed by the on-device model against the shortlist. Checked and probable — confirmed, but a second candidate was close. Unsure or unmatched — the model declined the shortlist, or nothing was retrieved; the row carries no values and blocks the Log button. Not checked — this iPhone has no Apple Intelligence, so the match is by name only.

Checking is not a nicety. Text search for “coffee” retrieves Kaffeepulver, löslich at 350 kcal/100 g alongside Kaffee, schwarz at 2, and a 250 g cup logged against the wrong one is 875 kcal instead of 5 — a 40% error on the whole day from one row. That is the failure the model's second pass exists to catch, and screen 03b is it being caught.

Five states did not survive contact with the sheet. What I could draw legibly is three tiers, carried by structure rather than colour: a settled row is one line with nothing to operate; a glance row is taller, names the runner-up it rejected, and carries a chevron and an open portion control; a blocking row has a dashed empty value slot and makes the Log button inert. Within a tier, the exact state is carried by a glyph and a sentence, which are read only once the eye is already on that row. “Not checked” is not a row state at all in this design — it is a property of the device, so it is lifted to one sentence above the Log button and a lower threshold for the glance tier. The reasoning, and what that compromise costs, is break 02.

05 — A normal day, and deviations from it

A proposal is not a log

Most days are the same day. Today can therefore offer the day's baseline before anything has been typed — but a proposal that is indistinguishable from a record destroys the integrity of every total, every average and everything written to Health. So the proposal is drawn as a separate dashed card above the entries, never as rows inside a meal slot, and never inside the headline figure.

06 — Trends

Three measures, one hue, no verdict

This screen reverses an explicit decision in PLAN.md ("no trend charts or history graphs"), so it carries the most regulatory risk in the app. Everything about it is built to stay descriptive.

On colour. The brand has one accent and the design rule says nutrient values are never tinted — so a three-series rainbow was never available, and neither was tangerine on the data. Energy, protein and fibre are drawn as small multiples: three charts, one measure each, one axis each, a single warm-graphite ink. No dual axes, no legend (a single series needs none), no series colour at all. Tangerine appears exactly once, on the selected range — the only thing on the screen you can operate. The coverage strip is the one place with more than one class, and it uses an ordinal ramp of the same hue, validated light and dark, not a second colour.

08 — User journeys

Measured against five and twenty seconds

Targets from PLAN.md: a repeat meal in under 5 s and 3 taps, a meal never logged before in under 20 s. Seconds below are wall-clock estimates including reading and typing, at roughly 28 words per minute of thumb typing — fast for a phone, slow for a keyboard. A tap is a discrete deliberate touch; typing is counted as time, not as taps. Where a journey misses, it says so.

The typed path cannot reach 5 seconds and never will: 25 characters is six seconds of thumb before anything else happens. That is the whole argument for phrase recall. One-line input is not fast; remembering one-line input is.

Comfortably inside the target, and the seconds are not the problem here. The target is met and the screen never looks broken. Two separate costs though, and only one of them is now covered. Match checking is handled honestly — the sheet says “Matched by name” and moves both database rows into the glance tier (screen 03d), which adds roughly four seconds a day. Sentence parsing is not: nothing distinguishes “the parser is simpler on this device” from “you phrased it badly”, so a user on an older iPhone learns over weeks that the app does not understand them. That gap is worth closing with the same move — one sentence, stated as fact, next to the row it explains. See break 07.

A whole normal day in one tap is the fastest path in the product by a wide margin, and that is the problem with it. The cheaper accepting a day gets, the more the log drifts toward a record of the user's habits rather than their eating — and the 7-day average reads the same either way. The mock defends this with the assumed state and with keeping anything assumed out of Health, but a user who taps “Log all four” is logging, not assuming, and the app then has no way to tell the difference. The alternative — a one-tap accept that writes the day as assumed and offers to confirm it in the evening — is slower and more honest, and I would argue for it.

09 — Where the premise broke down

Nine places the design would not draw

These are the points where following the brief produced something I could not defend, or where two rules in the brief contradict each other. They are the most useful part of this document.

BREAK 01

“Usual” has no meaning the first time, and the bucket model knows it

Less / Usual / More is defined as 0.7× / 1× / 1.4× of what you last had. For a food never logged, there is no “what you last had”, so the multiplier has nothing to multiply. The sheet falls back to the database's own portion, which means the same control means two different things on two rows of the same sheet: on a recalled row, “Usual” is a measured fact about this user; on a first-time row it is a population average from Ciqual.

Drawn as: the first-time row spells out the grams underneath (“75 g · first time, read from ‘big spoon’”) and the recalled row does not. That is a patch, not a fix. The real fix is a different control for a first-time row — a portion picker, not a multiplier — which reintroduces the thing the premise removed.

Where to see it

Screen 03b, the Kimchi row.

BREAK 02

Five match states will not fit on one sheet; three tiers will

From history, checked-and-certain, checked-and-probable, unsure, and not-checked are five states, and I could not make five legible without reaching for colour, which is forbidden, or for five badges, which is worse. What works is three tiers with structure doing the encoding: settled (one line, nothing to operate), glance (taller, chevron, open portion control, runner-up named), blocked (dashed empty value, Log inert). The remaining distinctions ride a glyph and a sentence inside a tier, which are read only after the eye has already landed.

Three things fall out of that, and a reviewer should push on all of them. First, from-history and checked-certain are drawn the same way and differ only in a sentence. That is deliberate — a user scanning a settled sheet has no decision to make either way — but it does mean the sheet cannot answer “which of these did the model touch?” at a glance. Second, the only state with a reliable at-a-glance signal is blocked, because it is the only one that changes something global. Probable rows are found by height, and height is a weak signal in a four-row list and a weaker one at accessibility type sizes, where every row is tall. The count above the Log button (“1 row worth a glance”) exists to cover that, and it is the single load-bearing element of this design. Third, not-checked is not a row state at all here. It applies to every eligible row at once, so encoding it per row doubles the vocabulary for nothing; it became one sentence and a lower glance threshold. If a device can ever check some rows and not others, that decision collapses.

Where to see it

Screens 03a–03d, in that order, are the tier system under increasing load.

BREAK 03

Fibre survives ±20% as a day total and does not survive it as a trend

Energy at ±15–20% is fine: a 400 kcal error on 2,300 moves the 7-day line by 57 kcal, inside the noise. Fibre does not behave that way. A single lentil portion is 8–10 g of fibre, a third of a typical day, so one bucket chosen wrong moves the day by 12% and a week of the same habit moves the rolling average by more than the signal anyone is looking for.

The brief names energy, protein and fibre as co-equal headlines. On the evidence of drawing the chart, they are not co-equal: the fibre chart is the one where the rolling average is mostly reporting bucket choices. I drew it anyway, with the same restraint as the others, but it is the first thing I would cut, or the first thing I would give a wider visible band.

BREAK 04

“Incompleteness is first-class” and “no judgement” pull in opposite directions

Making incompleteness visible means counting it, and every count of logged versus missing is one formatting decision away from a score. “3 of 4 meals” is a fraction. A progress bar over the day's slots is a completion metric. A coverage percentage on Trends is a grade for the user's diligence rather than their diet — which is arguably worse, because it is a grade the app will absolutely deliver and the regulatory line was drawn around nutrition, not behaviour.

Drawn as: coverage is always a sentence naming what is there (“Breakfast and lunch logged. Dinner assumed. Nothing for the evening.”) and never a ratio, and the Trends strip shows days without summarising them. The strip's subtitle states a count because an average has to say what it covers — that is the one number I could not avoid, and it is deliberately about the data, not the user.

BREAK 05

Removing the meals library removed the tab bar

The original brief had a Meals tab. With no curation, there is nothing to put in it: recall is invisible, and a screen listing remembered lines that you can log from is a meals library wearing a different hat. So Meals is gone, and so is Library, since custom foods and recipes were mostly a way to build meals.

What is left is Today, Trends, Settings — three tabs, which happens to satisfy the plan's “three tabs, no more”, but one of them is Settings, which is a weak third destination. The honest shape of this app is now one screen with a chart attached, and a tab bar is arguably the wrong chrome for it. I drew the tab bar because Trends has to be reachable in one tap from Today for J5 to work. A segmented control in the Today navigation bar would also do it.

BREAK 06

A proposal that is cheap to accept stops being a proposal

The baseline card is drawn to be unmistakably not-logged: dashed, outside the sections, excluded from the headline figure. All of that holds right up to the moment the user taps “Log all four”, at which point a day nobody ate becomes a day of logged entries indistinguishable from a day they typed — and it is written to Health.

One tap for a whole day is the fastest path in the product, so it will be the most-used one, so the modal day in the database will be a day nobody actually recorded. The design cannot prevent this; it can only make the distinction visible for as long as it is true. The alternative worth arguing about is accepting the baseline as assumed rather than logged, which keeps it out of Health and keeps it marked in Trends, at the cost of being slower and less satisfying. See J7.

BREAK 07

“Looks normal” and “is honest about what it did” were in conflict, and honesty won

The original rule was that the deterministic path must look normal rather than broken: no badge, no banner, no asterisk. Drawn that way, a user without Apple Intelligence gets worse matches and worse parses forever and has no way to learn why. They conclude the app does not understand them, which is true, and that it is badly made, which is the wrong conclusion from the right evidence.

Screen 03d resolves it for matching, and the resolution is not a compromise: the row says “Matched by name” where a checked row says “Checked”. That is a plain description of what the app did, not a warning and not an apology, and the sheet's structure, order, controls and copy pattern are unchanged. It satisfies both rules at once because it never claims anything is wrong. The lesson generalises: “looks normal” fails only when the honest version is phrased as a defect.

What is still unresolved is parsing. Screen 10 shows a sentence the fallback cannot read, and the only tell there is a phrasing hint addressed to the user — advice about their wording rather than a fact about the device. The same move would fix it, and one line in Settings, said once where the user agreed to it, is what DESIGN.md asks for anyway.

Where to see it

Screen 03d against 03b; screen 10 for the part that is still open.

BREAK 08

The range switcher changes what the chart means

The premise is a 7-day rolling average read over months. A Week range cannot show that — seven days is one point. So Week shows daily columns instead, which is a different statistic answering a different question, behind a control that looks like a zoom.

Drawn as: the subtitle changes with the range and says which it is (“7-day average” versus “daily totals”), and the mark changes from a line to columns so the switch is visible rather than implied. It works, but a reviewer should know that one control is doing two jobs. Dropping Week entirely is a defensible alternative, and it is what the premise actually implies.

BREAK 09

Two primary actions want the same thumb

The plan requires primary actions at the bottom. The composer is pinned at the bottom of Today; the resolution sheet's Log button is pinned at the bottom of the sheet; dictation's hold-to-talk is pinned at the bottom too. Each is right on its own screen, and the result is three different things living at the same coordinate in a sequence that takes four seconds.

The risk is a mislog: Log sits roughly where the composer's mic was a second earlier. Drawn as: the sheet's Log button is full width while the composer's controls are not, so the target changes shape as well as function, and the sheet animates up rather than appearing. That is mitigation, not a solution. It needs testing on a device before anyone trusts it, and it is the one thing in this document a prototype would settle in an afternoon.

Food names and composition follow the generic European tables the app bundles — Ciqual and the German BLS — so names read as “Apfel, mit Schale, roh” rather than as brands. Nutrient values are rounded from those tables: oats ≈ 370 kcal/100 g, banana ≈ 90, black coffee ≈ 2, rye bread ≈ 230, butter ≈ 750, linseed oil ≈ 884. The 90 days of Trends data are synthetic and clearly marked as a sample; nothing here is anyone's record.

Chart palette: a single warm-graphite hue, two ordinal steps plus an empty track, validated against the card surface in both appearances (light 2.53:1, dark 3.68:1 for the lighter step; monotone lightness; hue spread 2°). No categorical palette is used because no chart carries more than one series.

Mocks only. No Swift source was touched, and nothing here is a commitment.
